What is Phishing?

The Attack Vector

Phishing is a form of social engineering where attackers impersonate trustworthy entities (banks, service providers, colleagues) via digital communication to manipulate victims into revealing sensitive information.

3.4B
Phishing Emails Sent Daily Globally

Critical Objectives

Attackers construct phishing lures to bypass structural firewalls by targeting the human element. They seek to extract:

  • User Credentials (Passwords, SSO tokens, security codes)
  • Financial Assets (Credit card numbers, corporate bank info)
  • Personally Identifiable Information (PII)
  • System Entry (Installing malware, ransomware, or spyware)

The Attack Lifecycle

01
Lure Design

Attacker drafts email mimicking trusted company.

02
Delivery

Email bypasses filter, landing in inbox.

03
Trigger (User)

Victim clicks link, inputs sensitive data.

04
Exfiltration

Attacker steals credentials and invades networks.

Common Attack Variations

Phishing is not a single technique. Attackers modify their delivery vector depending on the target parameters.

Spear Phishing

Highly targeted attacks crafted for a specific individual or team.

Unlike broad campaigns, spear phishing relies on pre-attack reconnaissance. Attackers gather personal details from social media (LinkedIn, Twitter) to construct credible scenarios targeting your specific role, projects, or organization.

High Risk: Extremely hard to spot.
Read Deep Dive

Whaling

Targets high-profile executives like CEOs, CFOs, or Board members.

Whaling attempts to extract large wire transfers, trade secrets, or high-privilege system keys. The emails mimic legal subpoenas, board complaints, or urgent industry regulatory threats requiring action from top leadership.

Financial Threat: Targets cash flow directly.
Read Deep Dive

Smishing (SMS)

Phishing attacks performed via SMS / text messages.

Attackers send texts claiming you have a missed delivery package, unpaid toll bills, or a compromised bank account. Victims are pressured to click shortened links (`bit.ly`, `tinyurl`) which download malware or load spoofed credential sites on mobile screens.

Mobile Risk: Mobile browsers hide URL details.
Read Deep Dive

Vishing (Voice)

Phishing conducted through voice phone calls.

Attackers use caller ID spoofing and synthetic AI voice cloning to impersonate IT support, banking fraud departments, or tax officers. They create intense urgency to extract verbal passwords, MFA codes, or access to your computer.

AI Spoofing: Voice cloning makes verification harder.
Read Deep Dive

Clone Phishing

Cloning a legitimate, previously sent email and swapping attachments or links.

The attacker intercepts or obtains a copy of a legitimate email with attachments or links. They create an almost identical duplicate, replacing the safe attachment with malware or editing the link, claiming it is an "updated version" or "resubmission".

Trust Exploited: Leverages trust of real transactions.

  • Domain typosquatting (micros0ft with a '0' zero)

01

Inspect the Base Domain

Ensure the base domain strictly matches the official website (e.g. paypal.com NOT paypal-security.com).

02

Look for IDN Homograph Attacks

Attackers use international characters that look identical to standard letters (e.g., Cyrillic 'а' instead of Latin 'a') to trick filters and users.

03

Never Trust Aesthetics Alone

Modern browser cloning tools allow attackers to duplicate CSS templates in seconds. A beautiful interface does not equal safety.

Social Engineering Tactics

Phishing is not just a technology hack—it is a human psychology hack. Attackers use emotional triggers to bypass logical skepticism.

1. Urgency & Panic

"Act within 24 hours or your accounts will be deleted!"

By placing strict time limits, attackers trigger cortisol responses in victims, forcing fast actions before rational cognitive evaluation takes place.

2. Authority & Fear

"This is IRS / Law Enforcement / Corporate Legal Service."

Impersonating figures of authority (CEOs, government agents, internal HR leaders) exploits our natural psychological tendency to comply with instructions to avoid punishment.

3. Greed & Reward

"Congratulations! You won the company bonus/gift card."

Dangling monetary gains, free gifts, or exclusive system access appeals to excitement. Victims bypass security checks to claim the "reward."

4. Curiosity & Intrigue

"View confidential leaked documents here."

Leverages natural curiosity. Using subjects like "Leaked payroll roster" or "Candid office pictures" baits employees into clicking files.

Real-World Security Breaches

Phishing is not a theoretical hazard. These real incidents demonstrate the catastrophic costs of failed threat detection.

FACC CEO Impersonation

$54 Million Lost

An aerospace component manufacturer fell victim to a Whaling attack. The attacker sent an email disguised as the company CEO, requesting an urgent wire transfer for a secret acquisition project. The transaction was approved and processed, leading to the CEO's termination.

Key Lesson: Establish strict out-of-band verification (like a phone call or face-to-face meet) for financial routing changes.

Sony Pictures Breach

100TB data leaked

Attackers targeted Sony executives with highly realistic Spear Phishing emails. Disguised as security warnings, the emails lured executives into inputting administrative login keys. The hackers gained access to servers, stealing unreleased movies and personal emails.

Key Lesson: Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) must be mandated across all entry portals to stop stolen keys from working.

Target HVAC Exploit

40M Credit Cards Stolen

Attackers phished a third-party air conditioning (HVAC) vendor working with Target. Once the vendor's billing account was phished, attackers used those credentials to infiltrate Target’s internal network, pivot to the point-of-sale systems, and steal data.

Key Lesson: Supply chain risks are real. Segment networks so vendors cannot access sensitive customer databases.

Prevention & Defense Strategies

Developing a strong human firewall is our most effective security line of defense. Remember these active protocols:

1. Mandate Multi-Factor (MFA)

MFA is the single most critical block. Even if attackers trick you into entering your password, they cannot gain entry without your physical device token.

2. Hover Before Clicking

Hover your mouse over every link to view the actual destination URL in the status bar. If it doesn't match the company domain, don't click it.

3. Out-of-Band Verification

Received an urgent request for money or confidential files? Confirm it via an independent pathway. Call the sender on a known phone number.

4. Avoid Public Workspaces

Never log into corporate portals using unencrypted public Wi-Fi networks. Attackers can execute man-in-the-middle exploits to steal keys.

Emergency Response Actions

If you accidentally click a link or input credentials on a suspicious portal, time is your enemy. Follow this incident response protocol:

01

Isolate the System

Disconnect your device from internet networks immediately. Unplug the Ethernet cable or turn off Wi-Fi to stop malware from contacting the attacker's server.

02

Update Credentials

Use a separate, secure device to change the passwords of the compromised account. If you reuse that password elsewhere, change those passwords immediately.

03

Alert IT Security

Report the incident to the Security Operations Center (SOC) or IT Helpdesk. Provide the phishing email headers, the spoofed URL, and the attachment details.

04

Monitor & Scan

Run a comprehensive antivirus scan on the isolated system. Check account activity logs for unauthorized logins or unexpected email forwarding rules.
